# 1.3 — Problem Framing & Value Creation

**Status:** Working draft — problem hypotheses require validation

## Working problem frame
Medicine-related information and routine coordination can involve multiple people, changing context, and details that are difficult to keep organized or communicate consistently. Families may rely on memory, paper, labels, messages, or separate personal systems.

This is a plausible problem frame, not proof that all families experience it, that it is severe enough to motivate adoption, or that Renomedy is the best solution. Research must establish who experiences which part, how often, what they do today, and what consequences they report.

## Start with the job, not the feature
A useful research prompt is: When someone is responsible for keeping medicine-related information organized or helping a relative coordinate care, what are they trying to get done, what makes it difficult, and what do they currently rely on?

Potential jobs to investigate:
- Keep relevant medicine-routine information in one understandable place.
- Prepare accurate context for a conversation with a clinician or pharmacist.
- Help family members stay aligned about practical coordination tasks.
- Find information when needed rather than reconstruct it from memory.
- Understand which information is current and which needs confirmation.

These are research prompts, not claims that Renomedy currently performs these jobs.

## Candidate sources of friction
Treat these as hypotheses, not universal truths:
- Information may be spread across several places.
- The person who knows details may not be the person coordinating an appointment.
- Family members may have different levels of access or understanding.
- Instructions and context may change over time.
- People may be uncertain which information is reliable or current.
- Existing tools may feel burdensome or poorly matched to family coordination.

Research should seek concrete recent examples and counterexamples. Avoid leading questions and do not treat polite interest as willingness to adopt or pay.

## Proposed value hypothesis
**Draft hypothesis:** If Renomedy helps a defined user group organize and review medicine-routine information with relevant family context, that group may experience less coordination friction and find it easier to prepare for care conversations.

Test for relevance, frequency, current alternatives, switching cost, trust, comprehension, safety, and repeat use.

## Value creation versus value capture
Value creation is the user benefit the product may provide, such as clearer organization or easier coordination. Value capture is the sustainable business mechanism that may allow the company to keep delivering that benefit. A meaningful problem does not automatically produce a viable business. Adoption, willingness to pay, acquisition cost, support burden, privacy expectations, and alternatives require separate evidence.

## How to test
A first research round of roughly 8–12 exploratory interviews has been proposed as a starting point, not a representative sample or guarantee of validation. Ask participants about the last time the problem occurred, what they did, what was frustrating, what they trusted, what they would not share, and what they tried already. Avoid collecting unnecessary sensitive medical details.

Record the segment and context, recent behavior, reported frequency and consequence, current tools, adoption barriers, trust concerns, and contradictory evidence.

## What not to infer
Do not infer market size from a few interviews, product-market fit from positive feedback, willingness to pay from compliments, or clinical benefit from perceived convenience.

## Owner decisions
- Choose the first problem to investigate.
- Choose the first audience for interviews.
- Define what evidence is enough to proceed to a prototype test.
- Decide what research data to retain and for how long.
