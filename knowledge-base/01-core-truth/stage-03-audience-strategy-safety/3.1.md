# 3.1 — Audience Evidence and Segment Hypotheses

**Status:** Working draft — owner approval pending

## What is supported
The source register and fact ledger record that the public website names parents, adult children, and family caregivers as intended audiences. This supports only the narrow claim that these groups are named in the recorded public concept framing. It does not prove demand, urgency, willingness to pay, adoption, or conversion.

## Segment hypotheses to test
1. **Adult children coordinating a parent's medicine routine.** Plausible because the public concept includes family coordination; actual pain severity and willingness to use Renomedy remain unvalidated.
2. **Parents or older adults organizing their own routines.** Named audience in the public source snapshot; specific needs, accessibility preferences, and adoption barriers are unknown.
3. **Other family caregivers.** Included in the broad concept; roles, responsibilities, and permissions vary and need research.
4. **People coordinating care across households.** A research hypothesis, not a confirmed target segment.

These are hypotheses, not ranked market facts. Do not state that one is the best or most profitable segment without comparative evidence.

## Research ethics
- Do not collect identifiable prescriptions or unnecessary sensitive health details in casual marketing interviews.
- Ask about routines and experiences rather than asking participants to disclose diagnoses or medication lists.
- Explain the research purpose, that participation is voluntary, and how notes will be used.
- Obtain appropriate consent; do not assume a caregiver can consent on behalf of another person.
- Do not promise product access, health outcomes, or compensation unless approved and real.
- An interview quote is not representative market evidence by itself.

## Evidence capture template
For each research session, record a pseudonymous participant code, date, recruitment method, relevant role, questions asked, notes where consent permits, observed behaviour, interpretation separated from observation, limitations, consent status, and follow-up. Store sensitive information only through an approved process.

## Questions that remain open
- Which segment experiences the most frequent and consequential coordination difficulty?
- What workaround do people use today?
- What triggers them to seek a solution?
- What makes a new medicine-related tool feel unsafe or burdensome?
- Who would decide, pay, set up, and maintain the tool?
- Which languages, accessibility needs, and household arrangements matter?
- What evidence would disconfirm the current preferred segment?
